Introduction to Regression

Before we start

Today we will use a regression line to make a prediction, interpret its slope, and inspect its mistakes. By the end, you should be able to use lm(), extract the coefficients, draw the fitted line, and explain what a residual means. You will need those skills for Lab 7.

Keep four questions in mind:

  • What does a regression line add to a correlation?
  • What does the slope mean in the units of our data?
  • How much better is the line than giving everyone the same prediction?
  • What can a convincing-looking line still get wrong?

Start with a prediction

Suppose you are helping an organization compare local labor markets. You know the share of adults in a county who have a college degree. How much does that tell you about the county’s household income?

Before looking at the figure, commit to an answer: If we compare counties with more college graduates to counties with fewer, what pattern do you expect? How much variation do you think will be left over?

There is a pattern. There is also a lot of room to be wrong. Regression gives us a way to describe both.

The data and its units

Our source is the U.S. Census Bureau’s 2020–2024 American Community Survey five-year estimates. These estimates combine five years of survey responses. Income amounts are expressed in 2024 inflation-adjusted dollars.

Download the prepared file:

  • county_education_income_2024.csv
  • Source tables, variable codes, and preparation notes

The file contains 3,144 counties and county equivalents in the 50 states and D.C. One county has no usable income estimate; our income examples use the remaining 3,143. All three regressions below use the same rows.

Variable Meaning Units
CollegePct Adults age 25+ with a bachelor’s degree or more Percent, from 0 to 100
IncomeK Median household income Thousands of 2024 dollars
PovertyPct Residents below the poverty threshold, among those for whom poverty status is determined Percent
MedianAge Median age of residents Years
Population Total population estimate People
CountyFIPS County identifier A five-character code, including leading zeros

One row is a county, not a person. A median describes the middle household in that county. It does not say that every household receives that income. Our unweighted regressions give each county the same weight, so a small county counts just as much as Wayne County.

Give the line a job

If we knew nothing about a county, we could predict the same value for every county: the average of the county median incomes, about $67,976. This is our baseline. It is not the national median household income.

Knowing CollegePct gives us a chance to do better. The regression line lets the prediction change with education.

From Tuesday’s lecture, we have a formula for the slope and intercept:

\[ \widehat{\beta}_1 = r\frac{s_Y}{s_X}, \qquad \widehat{\beta}_0 = \bar{Y}-\widehat{\beta}_1\bar{X}. \]

The correlation gives the direction and strength of the linear association. The ratio of standard deviations puts the slope back into the units of Y per unit of X.

We can translate the formulas directly into R:

line_by_hand <- counties %>%
  summarize(
    mean_x = mean(CollegePct),
    mean_y = mean(IncomeK),
    sd_x = sd(CollegePct),
    sd_y = sd(IncomeK),
    r = cor(CollegePct, IncomeK)
  ) %>%
  mutate(
    slope = r * sd_y / sd_x,
    intercept = mean_y - slope * mean_x
  )

line_by_hand %>% select(r, intercept, slope)
# A tibble: 1 × 3
      r intercept slope
  <dbl>     <dbl> <dbl>
1 0.720      37.3  1.24

The line passes through the point \((\bar{X}, \bar{Y})\). That is why the intercept formula subtracts \(\widehat{\beta}_1\bar{X}\) from \(\bar{Y}\).

Fit the same line with lm()

We do not need to repeat that arithmetic every time. R’s lm() fits the line for us:

education_model <- lm(IncomeK ~ CollegePct, data = counties)
coef(education_model)
(Intercept)  CollegePct
      37.27        1.24

Read the formula from left to right:

  • IncomeK is the response, or Y: the quantity we want to predict.
  • ~ means “modeled as a function of.”
  • CollegePct is the predictor, or X.
  • data = counties tells R where to find the columns.

We say that income is regressed on education. Swapping the two variables asks a different question and fits a different line. Correlation is symmetric; regression chooses an outcome.

The object education_model stores the fitted model. coef() extracts its coefficients:

b0 <- coef(education_model)[["(Intercept)"]]
b1 <- coef(education_model)[["CollegePct"]]

c(intercept = b0, slope = b1)
intercept     slope
    37.27      1.24

These match our hand calculation. In our units, the fitted equation is approximately

\[ \widehat{\text{IncomeK}} = 37.27 + 1.244\times\text{CollegePct}. \]

The hat means fitted or predicted. We are using a straight line to approximate the conditional mean of income given education. We have estimated that line from data; we have not discovered an exact law for every county.

Interpret it in dollars

A one-unit increase in CollegePct means one percentage point, such as going from 30% to 31%. It does not mean increasing the share by 100%.

Our slope says:

Counties with a college share one percentage point higher have fitted median household income about $1,244 higher, on average in this linear model.

That is an association across counties. It is not the effect of giving one resident a degree.

Example 1: A ten-point difference

Going from 30% to 40% is a ten-percentage-point difference. Multiply the slope by ten:

10 * b1 * 1000
[1] 12436

Example 2: Make a prediction with predict()

To predict income for a county with CollegePct = 30, substitute 30 into the fitted equation:

(b0 + b1 * 30) * 1000
[1] 74579

For several values at once, use predict(). The new data must have a column with the same predictor name used in the model:

new_counties <- tibble(CollegePct = c(30, 40))

new_counties %>%
  mutate(predicted_income_dollars =
           1000 * predict(education_model, newdata = new_counties))
# A tibble: 2 × 2
  CollegePct predicted_income_dollars
       <dbl>                    <dbl>
1         30                   74579.
2         40                   87015.

The model predicts in the units of its response. Since we fit IncomeK, predict() returns thousands of dollars. We multiply by 1,000 to report dollars.

What about the intercept?

The intercept is the fitted value at CollegePct = 0: about $37,271. Before giving it a substantive interpretation, check whether zero is represented in our data. The observed shares in this model range from about 0% to 80.5%.

Zero appears in one county in this snapshot: Loving County, Texas, whose estimated population is just 33. That is little support for a broad story about communities with no college graduates. Here, the intercept mainly helps position the line. In other datasets, zero may be outside the observed range altogether.

Example 3: Change the units, keep the relationship

Now fit the same model with income in dollars instead of thousands of dollars:

dollar_model <- lm(MedianIncome ~ CollegePct, data = counties)

coef(education_model)
(Intercept)  CollegePct
      37.27        1.24 
coef(dollar_model)
(Intercept)  CollegePct
      37271        1244

Always attach units to a coefficient. A slope of 1.244 and a slope of 1,243.6 can describe exactly the same relationship.

Look at the mistakes

A prediction is not an observation. For county \(i\), the residual is

\[ e_i = Y_i-\widehat{Y}_i. \]

  • A positive residual means observed income is above the line: we underpredicted.
  • A negative residual means observed income is below the line: we overpredicted.

R gives us fitted values with fitted() and residuals with resid():

county_results <- counties %>%
  mutate(
    fitted_income = fitted(education_model),
    residual = resid(education_model)
  )

county_results %>%
  filter(State == "Michigan",
         County %in% c("Ingham County", "Ottawa County", "Washtenaw County")) %>%
  select(County, IncomeK, fitted_income, residual)
# A tibble: 3 × 4
  County           IncomeK fitted_income residual
  <chr>              <dbl>         <dbl>    <dbl>
1 Ingham County       65.5          88.5   -23.0
2 Ottawa County       90.5          85.7     4.84
3 Washtenaw County    89.2         110.    -21.1

For Ingham, the model overpredicts income. Ottawa is above the line. What might a one-predictor model miss about the kinds of households living in these counties? About their industries, housing costs, or age distributions?

Those are questions for further analysis. The residual by itself does not tell us which explanation is correct.

Why this line? Squared errors

Residuals above and below a line can cancel. Squaring them makes every miss count positively and gives large misses more weight.

Ordinary least squares, or OLS, chooses the intercept and slope to minimize

\[ \text{SSE}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_i-\widehat{Y}_i)^2. \]

Here are three candidate slopes. For each slope, we position the line through the sample means, giving it the best intercept for that slope.

A convenient summary is root mean squared error:

\[ \text{RMSE} =\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_i^2}. \]

The baseline’s RMSE is about $17,772. The education model’s RMSE is about $12,338. These describe errors on the same data used to fit the model. Later we will ask how well a model does on observations it has not seen.

How much variation did we account for?

\(R^2\) compares the line’s squared errors with the baseline’s squared errors:

\[ R^2 =1-\frac{\sum_i(Y_i-\widehat{Y}_i)^2} {\sum_i(Y_i-\bar{Y})^2}. \]

sse_model <- sum(resid(education_model)^2)
sse_baseline <- sum((counties$IncomeK - mean(counties$IncomeK))^2)

1 - sse_model / sse_baseline
[1] 0.518
summary(education_model)$r.squared
[1] 0.518

Here, \(R^2\) is about 51.8%. The fitted line reduces squared error by that proportion relative to the baseline.

It does not mean that education causes that proportion of income, or that this proportion of counties is predicted exactly. For simple OLS with an intercept, \(R^2\) also equals the squared sample correlation.

Two more worked examples

Keep the outcome and the counties fixed. Change only the predictor.

Example 4: A negative slope

What do you expect if we regress median household income on the poverty rate?

poverty_model <- lm(IncomeK ~ PovertyPct, data = counties)
coef(poverty_model)
(Intercept)  PovertyPct
      98.80       -2.17

A one-percentage-point higher poverty rate is associated with about $2,167 lower fitted median household income.

Income and poverty are related descriptions of local economic conditions. This is a descriptive comparison, rather than a forecast made before we know anything about income.

Example 5: A predictor that tells us little

Maybe older counties have higher incomes because residents have had more time to accumulate earnings. Maybe retired households have lower incomes. Let’s check:

age_model <- lm(IncomeK ~ MedianAge, data = counties)
coef(age_model)
(Intercept)   MedianAge
     82.383      -0.344 
summary(age_model)$r.squared
[1] 0.011

There is a fitted slope. There is also very little reduction in squared error. R will fit a line even when the predictor is not especially useful.

Notice the poverty model’s far-right tail: the fitted line drops below zero even though the observed county incomes are positive. It misses that part of the relationship badly. A respectable \(R^2\) does not guarantee that every prediction is sensible.

Explore several relationships

When we have several candidate variables, a scatterplot matrix gives us a first look at all their pairwise relationships.

county_numeric <- counties %>%
  select(CollegePct, IncomeK, PovertyPct, MedianAge)

ggpairs(
  county_numeric,
  columnLabels = c("College (%)", "Income ($1,000s)", "Poverty (%)", "Median age"),
  lower = list(continuous = wrap("points", alpha = 0.12, size = 0.4)),
  upper = list(continuous = wrap("cor", stars = FALSE, size = 4))
) +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12)

Read one pair at a time. The diagonal describes each variable’s distribution. The off-diagonal panels show pairs of variables. The upper panels summarize those pairs with correlations.

Don’t feed an ID code into cor() just because R stored it as a number. FIPS codes identify places. They are not measurements of how much “county” a place has.

The correlation matrix needs to become a long table with one row per pair. as.table() preserves the row and column labels; as.data.frame() puts them into columns.

cor_long <- as.data.frame(as.table(county_cor)) %>%
  rename(variable_x = Var1, variable_y = Var2, correlation = Freq) %>%

ggplot(cor_long, aes(variable_x, variable_y, fill = correlation)) +
  geom_tile(color = "white") +
  geom_text(aes(label = round(correlation, 2))) +
  scale_fill_gradient2(low = "#D9A27F", mid = "white", high = "#8DB4A3",
                       limits = c(-1, 1)) +
  coord_equal() +
  labs(x = NULL, y = NULL) +
  theme_minimal()

The ordering check keeps one triangle and removes the diagonal. The numbers and signs matter more than choosing an elaborate plotting style.

Four ways to fool yourself

These are short examples to return to as you work. We do not need a complicated model to make a complicated mistake.

1. A small correlation does not rule out a pattern

Correlation summarizes a linear relationship. Always look at the scatterplot before deciding that a small correlation means there is nothing to learn.

NoteTRY IT: Predict before calculating

For the U-shaped example, imagine increasing X from a very negative value toward zero, and then from zero toward a very positive value. How does Y change on each part of the picture? Why does one straight slope struggle?

2. A large residual is not the same as influence

When you see an unusual point, inspect it. Check the units, the source, and what the observation represents. “The regression looks nicer without it” is not a data-cleaning rule.

3. Extrapolation is easy to request and hard to defend

Before reporting a prediction, compare the requested X with the X values used to fit the model:

range(counties$CollegePct)
[1]  0.0 80.5

Also ask whether the new observation is comparable to the ones we used. A county-level model is not a salary calculator for an individual graduate.

4. Our coefficients depend on the sample

Recall last week’s discussion of sampling variation. A slope is calculated from the observations we happen to use. Change those observations and the estimated slope changes.

You can produce one of those smaller-sample fits yourself:

set.seed(15)

small_sample <- counties %>% slice_sample(n = 50)
small_model <- lm(IncomeK ~ CollegePct, data = small_sample)

coef(small_model)
(Intercept)  CollegePct
      31.75        1.52 
coef(education_model)
(Intercept)  CollegePct
      37.27        1.24

We will return to uncertainty about coefficients. For now, remember that a coefficient is an estimate built from data.
